## Source 1: The Mental Model Fallacy

- **URL:** https://commoncog.com/the-mental-model-fallacy/
- **Date:** Published 13 Nov 2018, updated 8 May 2026
- **Credibility:** primary
- **Core arguments [He said]:**
  - Reading descriptions of mental models written by non-practitioners is mostly a waste of time.
  - The most valuable mental models are tacit (he uses the Greek *technê*). They come from practice and do not survive being written down.
  - Thought experiment: if Buffett and Munger wrote down all they know, a student with that list would still lose to them, because practice matters.
  - Critique of Farnam Street: wrong "resolution" for practitioners, posts lack actionable handles. Ask of any post "how is this useful, how do I test it?"
  - Prescription: read source material from master practitioners, copy their actions, "climb their skill trees", and reflect through trial and error.
  - Points forward to "A Framework for Putting Mental Models to Practice" as the constructive version.
- **Quotes:**
  - "The most valuable mental models do not survive codification." (Chin)
  - "words are just hints when it comes to technê" (Chin)
  - "Don't read third-party accounts of technê." (Chin)
  - "Read from the source material of master practitioners" (Chin)

## Source 2: Why Tacit Knowledge is More Important Than Deliberate Practice

- **URL:** https://commoncog.com/tacit-knowledge-is-a-real-thing/
- **Date:** Published 9 Jun 2020, updated 8 May 2026 (Part 1 of the Tacit Knowledge series)
- **Credibility:** primary
- **Core arguments [He said]:**
  - Tacit knowledge is knowledge that cannot be captured in words alone (bike riding is his example). It is learned through imitation, emulation and apprenticeship.
  - Expert explanations turn into long lists of caveats and exceptions (examples: a software tech lead, Atul Gawande on appendectomies).
  - Turning tacit knowledge into explicit rules is very hard, so stop trying and pursue tacit knowledge directly.
  - Deliberate practice (Ericsson) works only in fields with established teaching methods (music, maths, chess). Most knowledge work is not like that.
  - Naturalistic Decision Making (NDM) research is more useful for business and career practitioners. Gary Klein's 1970s work with Air Force aircraft maintenance officers: their judgement could not be turned into procedures.
  - Practical advice: find a master, work under them, learn by "emulation, feedback, and osmosis". Use NDM methods such as the Critical Decision Method.
  - Pragmatic stance: whether tacit knowledge *can* ultimately be made explicit does not matter, act as if it is real.
- **Sources he cites:** Gary Klein (*Sources of Power*), K. Anders Ericsson (*Peak*), NDM community, Cambridge and Oxford Handbooks of Expertise, Gawande, Samo Burja, Gladwell, John Boyd, Andy Matuschak ("transmissionism").
- **Quotes:**
  - "deliberate practice can only exist in fields like music and math and chess" (Chin)
  - "the way to learn Hieu's techniques was to copy him" (Chin, Vietnam office anecdote)
  - "We should act as if tacit knowledge were a fact" (Chin)

## Source 3: A Small Update to Commonplace's Principles

- **URL:** https://commoncog.com/a-small-update-to-commonplaces-principles/
- **Date:** Published 3 Jun 2019, updated 8 May 2026
- **Credibility:** primary
- **Core arguments [He said]:**
  - Original blog principle #1: take an idea that seems useful, try it in his career, write up results. #2: bend writing toward usefulness. #3: "mix things up" (~20% less-practical material).
  - He drops #3, admitting it is neither useful nor true and that he did not live by it.
  - New principle: epistemic humility and rigour. Confident language only inside his circle of competence.
  - Evidence hierarchy for practitioner advice: top tier = "believable" experts who are exposed to negative outcomes, next = testing the technique in your own life (the best form of anecdotal evidence). For science: distrust single studies and underpowered significance.
  - Calls his self-testing his "skin in the game" (Taleb's phrase, used without naming Taleb on the page per extraction).
- **Quotes:**
  - "I've come to realise that principle #3 isn't particularly useful, nor true." (Chin)
  - "we cannot truly know the truth, but we can inch ever closer to it" (Chin)
  - "Consider this my skin in the game." (Chin)

## Source 4: Becoming Data Driven in Business (series intro)

- **URL:** https://commoncog.com/becoming-data-driven-in-business/
- **Date:** Published 15 Apr 2023, updated 19 Aug 2024
- **Credibility:** primary
- **Core arguments [He said]:**
  - Most people think "data driven" means looking at charts daily. It actually needs deeper skills and organisational capacity.
  - Amazon's metrics discipline traces back to Statistical Process Control (Shewhart, Deming after the war). He did not expect that link.
  - To be data driven, you must understand variation. Process behaviour charts (XmR) separate routine from exceptional variation.
  - Knowledge (Deming's sense) = theories or models that let you predict better. Operational rigour is the pursuit of knowledge.
  - Assuming no stable truths in business is a useful working hack.
  - Series came from a 2022 project with ex-Amazon executives Colin Bryar and Bill Carr, ends with the Amazon Weekly Business Review (WBR).
  - The series also links to "The Deming Paradox" and "The Limits of Operational Excellence", which qualify the enthusiasm.
- **Quotes:**
  - "Most people think being data driven means looking at charts on a daily basis." (Chin)
  - "in order to become data driven, SPC practitioners argue, you need to understand variation." (Chin)
  - "assuming that there are no stable truths in business is a pretty good hack." (Chin)

## Source 5: This One Can Make Money or Not?

- **URL:** https://commoncog.com/this-one-can-make-money-or-not/
- **Date:** Published 28 Jan 2026, updated 13 Feb 2026 (closes the Asian Conglomerate Series)
- **Credibility:** primary
- **Core arguments [He said]:**
  - The Singlish question "this one can make money or not?" is a filter for strategy analysis, commentary, events and social media activity.
  - Being "commercial" = wanting to make money over the long term (borrowing Graham Duncan's framing: create more value than you capture, treat counterparties fairly because of repeat games).
  - Nearly all the Asian conglomerate founders studied were commercial, the main lesson is that wanting money helps when running a business.
  - Cash cows behind a moat give freedom to expand or to misallocate capital without immediate punishment (not unique to Asia).
  - Revenue-producing work (fixing a sales pipeline) beats prestige projects and commentary.
  - Commercial instinct is learnable (Munger became more commercial later in life), he says he is not the most commercial person he knows.
  - Weak cases (Liem Sioe Liong, Chung Ju-yung) might be better treated as "contingently true" narratives that update with new information.
- **Quotes:**
  - "this one can make money or not?" (Chin, quoting the Singlish phrase)
  - "it helps to want to make money when you're running a business." (Chin)
  - "So the secret is to keep at it until you get lucky." (on page, speaker attribution not confirmed)
